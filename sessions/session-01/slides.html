Definition $P \Longrightarrow Q$ asserts that there is no case in which $P$ holds and $Q$ fails. When $P$ is false the implication is true — vacuously.

Refresher · 1

Every rule is a theorem with a hypothesis

Laws of exponents For $a > 0$ and all real $m, n$: $$a^m a^n = a^{m+n} \qquad (a^m)^n = a^{mn} \qquad a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n}$$
Why $a^0 = 1$ Put $n = 0$ in the first law: $a^m a^0 = a^m$. As $a \neq 0$ we may divide by $a^m$, giving $a^0 = 1$. It is forced by the law, not chosen for convenience.
Trap $\sqrt{x^2} = x$ is false. The radical returns the non-negative root, so $\sqrt{x^2} = |x|$. Test $x = -3$.
Worked $\dfrac{8^{2/3}\cdot 2^{-1}}{4^{1/2}} = \dfrac{4 \cdot \tfrac12}{2} = 1$
The $a^0$ box is the slide. Derive it live: most of them were told $a^0=1$ as a rule to memorise, and seeing it fall out of a law in one line is the first small shock of the course. Then stress $a > 0$.
Activity 1

Spot the missing hypothesis

Activity 1groups of three12 min
Every statement below is false as written. For each one, give a counterexample, then repair it by adding the smallest hypothesis that makes it true — or argue that it cannot be repaired.
  1. $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$
  2. $\sqrt{x^2} = x$
  3. $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\,\sqrt{b}$
  4. $\log(a+b) = \log a + \log b$
  5. $\dfrac{1}{a+b} = \dfrac{1}{a} + \dfrac{1}{b}$
Share: each group presents one repair and the counterexample that forced it.
Three are repairable (1, 2, 3 — all need a non-negativity condition); 4 and 5 are not, and noticing that difference is the point. Do not give the answers: let a group defend a wrong repair and let the room break it with a number. That is a proof in miniature.
Refresher · 2

Absolute value is distance

Definition $$|x| = \begin{cases} x & x \geq 0 \\ -x & x < 0\end{cases}$$ Read $|a - b|$ as the distance from $a$ to $b$ on the line.
Proposition For all real $x, y$ and every $c > 0$: $$|xy| = |x||y| \qquad |x| \leq c \iff -c \leq x \leq c$$ $$|x + y| \leq |x| + |y| \quad \text{(triangle inequality)}$$
Worked $|2x - 3| < 5$ says $2x$ is within $5$ of $3$, so $-2 < 2x < 8$, that is $-1 < x < 4$. Every step is an equivalence.
Prove the triangle inequality on the board — it is two lines by squaring, and it is the first real proof they watch in this course. Say that it reappears in Session 11 for vectors, in exactly the same form.
Activity 2

Distance on the line

Activity 2pairs10 min
Turn each sentence into an absolute-value inequality, solve it, and draw the solution set on a number line.
  1. $x$ is within $3$ of $7$.
  2. $x$ is at least $2$ away from $-1$.
  3. $2x$ is closer to $5$ than $1$ is.
Share: two pairs draw their number lines on the board; the room decides which endpoints are included, and says why.
The endpoints are the lesson: (1) and (2) are closed, (3) is open. Make them justify inclusion from the definition rather than from the shape of the symbol. Answers: $[4,10]$; $x \leq -3$ or $x \geq 1$; $2 < x < 3$.
Refresher · 3

Order is three axioms; the rest is theorems

Order axioms For all real $a, b, c$:
  1. exactly one of $a < b$, $a = b$, $b < a$ holds;
  2. $a < b \Longrightarrow a + c < b + c$;
  3. $a < b$ and $0 < c \Longrightarrow ac < bc$.
Everything else is derived Claim: $a < b \Longrightarrow -b < -a$. By (2) add $-a-b$ to both sides of $a < b$: $$a - a - b < b - a - b \;\Longrightarrow\; -b < -a$$ No new rule was needed. This is what "axiomatic approach" means.
This is the most academic slide of the day and it is worth ten minutes. The message: you were handed a list of inequality rules; there are only three, and the list was theorems. Session 5 makes this a method.

Activity 3

Always, sometimes, or never

Activity 3groups of three12 min
You are given $a < b$. Sort these moves into always legal, legal under a condition, or never legal. State the condition, or give a counterexample.
  1. Add $c$ to both sides.
  2. Multiply both sides by $c$.
  3. Square both sides.
  4. Take reciprocals of both sides.
  5. Multiply both sides by $x$, where $x$ is the unknown.
  6. Apply a strictly increasing function $f$ to both sides.
Share: each group defends one card. Where two groups disagree, the counterexample settles it at the board.
Cards 4 and 6 are the interesting ones. Reciprocals reverse the order only when $a$ and $b$ share a sign ($2 < 3$ gives $\tfrac12 > \tfrac13$, but $-1 < 2$ does not give $-1 > \tfrac12$). Card 6 is always legal and is the general principle behind cards 1 and 2 — if a group finds that, put it on the board.
Refresher · 4

Where the quadratic formula comes from

Derivation Start from $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with $a \neq 0$ and divide by $a$: $$x^2 + \tfrac{b}{a}x = -\tfrac{c}{a}$$ Complete the square on the left, using $x^2 + \beta x = \left(x + \tfrac{\beta}{2}\right)^2 - \tfrac{\beta^2}{4}$: $$\left(x + \tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^{2} = \frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2}$$ Take square roots — both of them — and subtract $\tfrac{b}{2a}$: $$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$$
What the discriminant decides $b^2 - 4ac > 0$: two real roots. $= 0$: one repeated root. $< 0$: none in $\mathbb{R}$ — which is exactly the gap Session 13 fills with $\mathbb{C}$.
Do every line on the board, slowly. Ask at the $\pm$ step why both roots survive — that is where the equivalence is, and where students silently drop a solution for the rest of their lives.
Activity 4

Derive it without us

Activity 4pairs12 min
  1. Solve $3x^2 - 12x + 7 = 0$ by completing the square. Do not use the formula.
  2. Now close your notes and redo the derivation for a general $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$.
  3. Write one sentence saying what $b^2 - 4ac$ tells you, and why it tells you that.
Share: one pair derives the general case at the board while the room checks that every step is an equivalence.
Answer to (1): $x = 2 \pm \tfrac{\sqrt{15}}{3}$. Most pairs will get the arithmetic and miss step 3 — the "why" is that the discriminant is what sits under the square root, so its sign decides whether a real root exists at all. Collect step 3 in writing; it is a rehearsal for how homework is marked.
Exit ticket · 10 minutes

On your own, before you leave

1 Solve $|3x + 1| \geq 4$ and name which steps are equivalences.
2 Find all real $x$ with $\dfrac{2}{x-1} \leq 1$, handling the sign of $x - 1$ explicitly.
3 Show $x^2 + y^2 \geq 2xy$ for all real $x, y$, and say exactly when equality holds.
Rule for today One sentence of justification under every answer. Hand it in on the way out — it is not graded, it tells us who to sit with tomorrow.
Individual, silent, ten minutes. Collect them: this is your diagnostic for the whole cohort and it costs nothing. Expected: (1) $x \leq -\tfrac53$ or $x \geq 1$; (2) $x < 1$ or $x \geq 3$; (3) $(x-y)^2 \geq 0$, equality iff $x = y$.
