Today's question

You have written $x^2$ a thousand times. Is $x^2$ a function?

And if it is — can you run it backwards?

Ask the box before you answer it, and let them commit out loud. Most of the room says yes. A more precise answer is "not completely specified — you have given me a rule but not its domain and codomain", and by the end of the first hour they will see that the same rule $x^2$ is four different functions depending on the sets you attach. Also flag the sets link: a function has a graph that is a special subset of $A \times B$, so yesterday was not a detour.

Concept I

A function is three things, not one

Definition A function $f : A \to B$ is three things at once:
  • a domain $A$
  • a codomain $B$
  • a rule giving each $a \in A$ exactly one $f(a) \in B$
Its graph is a subset of $A \times B$, with each $a$ occurring in exactly one pair.
Is $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $f(x) = x^2$. Every real has exactly one square, and it is real.
Is not

$g : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $g(x) = 1/x$: nothing at $x = 0$. Not total.

The relation $y^2=x$ on $\mathbb{R}$: two possible $y$ values when $x=4$, and none when $x=-1$. Not a function.

Say the two failure modes by name and write them on the board: not total (some input has no output) and not single-valued (some input has more than one). Every non-example for the rest of the course is one of these two. The $A \times B$ line is the payoff from yesterday — a function is literally a set of ordered pairs, and "exactly one" is a condition on that set.
Concept I

The codomain is declared; the image is computed

Definition $$f(A) = \{\, f(a) : a \in A \,\} \subseteq B$$ $$f^{-1}(S) = \{\, a \in A : f(a) \in S \,\}$$ The first is the image, the second the preimage of $S \subseteq B$.
Worked

$f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $f(x) = x^2$.

Codomain $\mathbb{R}$, image $[0, \infty)$. So $-1$ sits in the codomain but never in the image.

$f^{-1}(\{4\}) = \{-2, 2\}$, while $f^{-1}([-1, 0)) = \varnothing$.

Notation warning $f^{-1}(S)$ takes a set and returns a set. It exists for every function. It is not the inverse function $f^{-1}$ we meet after the break, which needs a bijection.
The one sentence to repeat: the codomain is a promise you make, the image is a fact you must prove. Note that $f^{-1}(S)$ is written with the same symbol as an inverse function but is a completely different object — it takes a set, returns a set, and needs no bijection to exist. Say that now; they will meet the real $f^{-1}$ in an hour and confuse the two otherwise.

Concept I

Injective, surjective, bijective — three questions about hitting

Definition

Injective: $f(a_1) = f(a_2) \Longrightarrow a_1 = a_2$. Nothing in $B$ is hit twice.

Surjective: $\forall b \in B\;\exists a \in A$ with $f(a) = b$. Nothing in $B$ is missed.

Bijective: both. Every $b$ hit exactly once.

Same rule, opposite answers

$f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $f(x) = x^2$: injective fails at $f(-2) = f(2)$; surjective fails at $-1$.

$f : [0,\infty) \to [0,\infty)$, $f(x) = x^2$: both hold. Nothing about the rule changed.

Draw the two arrow pictures on the board — dots in $A$, dots in $B$, arrows — before any symbols. Injective = no two arrows meet; surjective = no dot in $B$ is arrow-free. Then read the symbolic definitions back off the pictures. Stress that surjectivity is a statement about the codomain, so shrinking the codomain can create it out of nothing; that is what the second example does.
Concept I

Each of the four claims has one fixed opening move

The four templates

Injective: assume $f(a_1) = f(a_2)$, derive $a_1 = a_2$.

Not injective: exhibit one pair $a_1 \neq a_2$ with equal images.

Surjective: take arbitrary $b \in B$, construct an $a$ and check $f(a) = b$.

Not surjective: exhibit one $b$ and prove nothing maps to it.

Worked: $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $f(x) = 3x - 5$

Injective: $3a_1 - 5 = 3a_2 - 5 \Rightarrow 3a_1 = 3a_2 \Rightarrow a_1 = a_2$.

Surjective: given $y$, set $x = \tfrac{y+5}{3}$; then $f(x) = 3\cdot\tfrac{y+5}{3} - 5 = y$. So $f$ is a bijection.

The asymmetry is the lesson: the positive claims are universal and need an argument, the negative claims are existential and need one number. Students routinely try to disprove surjectivity by saying "it looks like nothing hits $-1$" — that is not the fourth template, and it costs marks all term. Note where the surjectivity witness $\tfrac{y+5}{3}$ came from: solve $f(x) = y$ on scrap paper, then present the answer as a construction. Say that out loud — the discovery and the write-up are different documents.

Composing, and undoing

Functions are things you can chain. Some chains can be reversed — and the condition for that is exactly the vocabulary from the first hour.

Concept II

Composition needs the codomain of one to be the domain of the next

Definition The composition of $f : A \to B$ with $g : B \to C$ is $$(g \circ f)(a) = g\big(f(a)\big), \qquad g \circ f : A \to C$$ Read it right to left: $f$ runs first. If the middle sets do not match, the composition does not exist.
Order is not a detail

$f(x) = x + 1$, $g(x) = x^2$, both $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$.

$(g \circ f)(x) = (x+1)^2 \qquad (f \circ g)(x) = x^2 + 1$

At $x = 1$: $4$ against $2$. So $g \circ f \neq f \circ g$.

The right-to-left reading trips everyone once; make them evaluate $(g \circ f)(3)$ out loud before you move on. The non-commutativity example is worth a beat — up to now every operation they have met (addition, multiplication, union, intersection) commuted, and this is the first that does not. Composition is associative, though: $(h \circ g) \circ f = h \circ (g \circ f)$, because both sides send $a$ to $h(g(f(a)))$. Say it; it is one line and they will use it silently forever.
Concept II

Injectivity of a chain is inherited by its first link

Theorem Let $f : A \to B$ and $g : B \to C$. If $g \circ f$ is injective, then $f$ is injective.
Proof Suppose $f(a_1) = f(a_2)$. Applying $g$ to both sides gives $g(f(a_1)) = g(f(a_2))$, that is $(g \circ f)(a_1) = (g \circ f)(a_2)$. Since $g \circ f$ is injective, $a_1 = a_2$. Hence $f$ is injective.
And $g$? Nothing follows. With $A = \{1\}$, $B = \{1,2\}$, $C = \{1\}$, $f(1) = 1$, $g(1) = g(2) = 1$: the chain is injective, $g$ is not.
This is boardExample 2 and it is the first proof of the day where the opening move is not obvious. Point out that the whole proof is the injectivity template with one extra step: apply $g$. Ask the room what happens if you try to prove the same thing about $g$ — someone will attempt it, and that failed attempt is exactly Activity 3, so let them start it and then cut them off.
Concept II

A function can be undone exactly when it is a bijection

Theorem $f : A \to B$ has a $g : B \to A$ with $g \circ f = \mathrm{id}_A$ and $f \circ g = \mathrm{id}_B$ if and only if $f$ is a bijection. This $g$ is unique: it is $f^{-1}$.
Proof ($\Longleftarrow$) Let $b \in B$. Surjectivity gives an $a$ with $f(a) = b$; injectivity says it is the only one. So $g(b) = a$ is well defined, and both composites are identities.
Trap $f^{-1}$ is not $\tfrac{1}{f}$. For $f(x) = 3x - 5$: $f^{-1}(1) = 2$, while $\tfrac{1}{f(1)} = -\tfrac12$.
Fix $f^{-1}$ undoes; $\tfrac1f$ divides. Same superscript, unrelated operations.
The $\Longleftarrow$ proof is the day's most important sentence: surjectivity gives existence, injectivity gives uniqueness, and together they give a well-defined rule. Write those two words on the board under the two hypotheses. Do the $\Longrightarrow$ direction live: if $g \circ f = \mathrm{id}$ then $f(a_1) = f(a_2)$ gives $a_1 = g(f(a_1)) = g(f(a_2)) = a_2$; if $f \circ g = \mathrm{id}$ then any $b$ equals $f(g(b))$, so $b$ is hit.
Activity 3

Find the broken step

Activity 3groups of three14 min
Claim. If $g \circ f$ is injective, then $g$ is injective. "Proof."

(i) Suppose $g(b_1) = g(b_2)$.
(ii) Write $b_1 = f(a_1)$ and $b_2 = f(a_2)$.
(iii) Then $(g \circ f)(a_1) = (g \circ f)(a_2)$, so $a_1 = a_2$.
(iv) Hence $b_1 = f(a_1) = f(a_2) = b_2$. $\blacksquare$

  1. Which of (i)–(iv) is the first unjustified line?
  2. Give a counterexample showing the claim is false.
  3. Add the smallest hypothesis on $f$ that repairs the proof.
  4. State and prove the companion result for $g \circ f$ surjective.
Share: one group presents the counterexample, another the repaired statement. The room decides if step 3 is the smallest fix.
The broken line is (ii): "write $b_1 = f(a_1)$" silently assumes every element of $B$ is in the image — that is surjectivity of $f$, and it was never given. Counterexample: the one from two slides ago, $f : \{1\} \to \{1,2\}$ with $g(1) = g(2) = 1$. Repair: assume $f$ surjective, and then the proof is correct as written. Step 4: if $g \circ f$ is surjective then $g$ is surjective — given $c \in C$ there is $a$ with $g(f(a)) = c$, so $f(a) \in B$ is a preimage. Note the pleasing symmetry: the chain hands injectivity down to the first link and surjectivity to the last.
Activity 3 · solutions

Step (ii) assumed what was never given

1 · The first unjustified line is (ii) "Write $b_1 = f(a_1)$" assumes every element of $B$ is an output of $f$ — that is surjectivity, never assumed.
2 · Counterexample $f : \{1\} \to \{1,2\}$, $f(1)=1$; and $g : \{1,2\} \to \{1\}$, $g(1)=g(2)=1$. Then $g \circ f$ is injective but $g$ is not.
3 · The repair Assume $f$ is surjective. Step (ii) is then legitimate and the rest stands.
4 · The companion result If $g \circ f$ is surjective, so is $g$: given $c \in C$, pick $a$ with $g(f(a)) = c$; then $f(a)$ is a preimage. $\;\blacksquare$
The symmetry is the thing to leave them with: a composition hands injectivity down to its first link and surjectivity up to its last. Both halves fail in the other direction, and the counterexample above is the smallest witness for one of them. If a group proposed "assume $g$ injective" as the repair in part 3, point out that this assumes the conclusion.
Concept II · trigonometry

Sine and cosine are coordinates, and the identity is Pythagoras

Definition From $(1,0)$, walk $\theta$ counter-clockwise along the unit circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$. The point you reach is $(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)$, and $\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$ where $\cos\theta \neq 0$. That distance is $\theta$ radians.
Why the identity is free The point lies on the circle, so $x^2 + y^2 = 1$. Substitute: $$\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$$ Not a new fact — the circle's equation, renamed.
Draw the circle before you say a word, and walk a finger round it. The single idea: $\theta$ is a length, so $\sin$ and $\cos$ are functions $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ like any other — not triangle ratios that stop at $90^\circ$. Derive $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$ by pointing at the circle; most of them memorised it and have never been shown that it is one substitution.
Concept II · trigonometry

Periodicity gives repeated outputs

$f$DomainImagePeriodInjective there?
$\sin$$\mathbb{R}$$[-1,1]$$2\pi$No ($\sin 0 = \sin \pi$)
$\cos$$\mathbb{R}$$[-1,1]$$2\pi$No ($\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta$)
$\tan$$\mathbb{R} \setminus \{\tfrac{\pi}{2} + k\pi\}$$\mathbb{R}$$\pi$No (period $\pi$)
Definition $p > 0$ is a period of $f$ if $f(\theta + p) = f(\theta)$ for every $\theta$. When there is a smallest positive one, it is the fundamental period.
The restriction trick, again $\sin$ cut down to $\left[-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right] \to [-1,1]$ is a bijection, so by the theorem it has an inverse. That inverse is $\arcsin$.
This is the slide that ties the two halves of the day together, so do not rush it. A periodic function repeats, so it is never injective on all of $\mathbb{R}$, so it has no inverse there — full stop. Every calculator's $\sin^{-1}$ button is silently working on a restricted domain, which is why it returns $-\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ to $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ and nothing else. Ask why we chose that interval and not, say, $[\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{3\pi}{2}]$: we could have, it is a convention, and the theorem does not care.
Watch out

The unit circle definition already fixed your units

Trap

$\sin(30) \neq \tfrac12$.

$\theta$ is an arc length, so $\sin(30) \approx -0.988$: the point after walking $30$ units round a circle of circumference $2\pi$. What you meant was $\sin\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{6}\right) = \tfrac12$.

Fix

Convert once, at the start: $\;\theta_{\text{rad}} = \dfrac{\pi}{180}\,\theta_{\deg}$.

Radians are the default here, in every calculus course, and in every programming language's standard library. Degrees are the special case that needs announcing.

Thirty seconds Convert $45^\circ$, $60^\circ$ and $270^\circ$ to radians. Then: what is $\cos(180)$, and what did whoever wrote it mean?
Worth thirty seconds of live demonstration: type Math.sin(30) somewhere and show the room $-0.988$. Every student loses marks to this at least once, and it is silent — the answer is a plausible number, just the wrong one. The deeper reason radians win is that they make $\sin$ a function of a length rather than of an arbitrary $1/360$ convention; that is what makes the derivative of $\sin$ come out as $\cos$ with no stray constant, which they will meet in calculus. Drill answers: $\tfrac{\pi}{4}$, $\tfrac{\pi}{3}$, $\tfrac{3\pi}{2}$; and $\cos(180) \approx -0.598$, where almost certainly $\cos(\pi) = -1$ was meant.
Activity 4

Derive the circle without us

Activity 4pairs12 min
Draw one unit circle. Close every other source — no tables, no phones.
  1. Place $\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{6}, \tfrac{\pi}{4}, \tfrac{\pi}{3}$ and read off $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ from the $30$–$60$–$90$ and $45$–$45$–$90$ triangles.
  2. From the picture alone, argue $\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta$ and $\sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta$.
  3. From the picture alone, argue $\sin(\theta + \pi) = -\sin\theta$.
  4. $2\pi$ is a period of $\tan$. Is it the smallest one? Justify.
Share: one pair draws the circle and defends step 4 at the board.
Answers: (1) $\left(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, \tfrac12\right)$, $\left(\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)$, $\left(\tfrac12, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)$. (2) reflecting in the $x$-axis fixes the first coordinate and negates the second. (3) adding $\pi$ sends the point to its antipode, negating both coordinates. (4) no — $\tan(\theta + \pi) = \tan\theta$, so $\pi$ is smaller, and it is in fact the smallest. Step 4 is the one they get wrong: "a period" and "the period" are different claims, and asking for the smallest is asking them to rule out everything below it, not just verify one value.
Activity 4 · solutions

Everything read off one picture

1 · The three angles

$\tfrac{\pi}{6}: \left(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\, \tfrac12\right)$  ·  $\tfrac{\pi}{4}: \left(\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2},\, \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)$  ·  $\tfrac{\pi}{3}: \left(\tfrac12,\, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)$

2 · Even and odd Negating $\theta$ reflects the point in the $x$-axis: the first coordinate is unchanged and the second flips. So $\cos(-\theta)=\cos\theta$, $\sin(-\theta)=-\sin\theta$.
3 · Adding $\pi$ Adding $\pi$ sends the point to its antipode, negating both coordinates. Reading off the second gives $\sin(\theta+\pi) = -\sin\theta$.

Part 4 is where the marks are. Confirming that $2\pi$ works says nothing about whether something smaller also works; the question asks them to rule out every $p$ below, which is a different and harder claim. Half the antipode argument in part 3 is already the proof that $\pi$ is a period of $\tan$ — if a pair spots that, they have connected the two halves of the activity themselves.

Wrap-up

What today was actually about

  • A rule is not a function. Domain and codomain are part of the object — change them and every answer changes.
  • Injective and surjective are claims to be proved, each with a fixed opening move.
  • A function is invertible exactly when it is a bijection — which is why $\arcsin$ needs a restricted domain.
