Warm-up

Start by describing what you count

Before you calculate, answer three questions
  1. What is one outcome? A code, a seating order, a committee, or a hand of cards?
  2. Does order matter? Is AB different from BA?
  3. Is repetition allowed? May an object be used more than once?

The point

The same objects can give different answers when the outcomes are different.

Use two names, A and B. Choosing a two-person committee gives one outcome, but choosing a president and a secretary gives two possible ordered outcomes. This distinction drives the rest of the lesson.

Sum rule

Sum rule: count one disjoint case or another

Definition Split the outcomes into two cases that do not overlap. If one case has $m$ outcomes and the other has $n$, then the total is $$m+n.$$ Cases that do not overlap are called disjoint.
Example A student chooses one elective. There are $3$ programming electives and $2$ design electives, and no course is in both lists. $$3+2=5$$ possible choices.
Stress the condition. If a course were listed in both groups, adding would count it twice. Inclusion–exclusion is the repair for overlapping cases, but it is beyond today's core material.
Product rule

Product rule: count a sequence of choices

Definition If a process has two stages:
  • $m$ choices for the first stage; and
  • for each first choice, $n$ choices for the second stage,

then there are

$$m\cdot n$$ complete outcomes.
Example With $3$ shirts and $4$ pairs of trousers, there are $$3\cdot4=12$$ outfits.
The phrase "for each first choice" matters. The rule still works if the number of second-stage choices changes, but then multiply the relevant branch sizes and add the disjoint branches.

Factorials

Factorials count arrangements

Definition For a positive integer $n$, $$n!=n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots2\cdot1.$$ It counts the ways to arrange all $n$ distinct objects in a row.
Example Five distinct books can be arranged in $$5!=5\cdot4\cdot3\cdot2\cdot1=120$$ orders.
The empty case By definition, $0!=1$. There is exactly one way to arrange zero objects: do nothing.
The word "distinct" is essential. Do not apply $n!$ unchanged when some objects are identical. The definition of $0!$ is useful for binomial coefficients later.
Permutations

Permutations: arrange some of the objects

Definition A permutation is a selection in which order matters. The number of ways to choose and order $r$ distinct objects from $n$ distinct objects is $$P(n,r)=n(n-1)\cdots(n-r+1)=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}.$$
Example From $6$ students, assign president, secretary, and treasurer: $$P(6,3)=6\cdot5\cdot4=120.$$
Why The roles are different, so order matters.
Read $P(6,3)$ as "permutations of 6 things taken 3 at a time." The formula assumes $0\le r\le n$ and distinct candidates.

Combinations

Combinations and binomial coefficients

Definition When order does not matter, the number of ways to choose $r$ objects from $n$ distinct objects is $$\binom{n}{r}=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!},\qquad 0\le r\le n.$$ It is read as "$n$ choose $r$."
Why divide Why divide by $r!$? In $P(n,r)$, each chosen group appears in $r!$ different orders.
Example ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, and CBA are the $3!=6$ orders of the same three-person group.
Link directly to the previous slide: $P(n,r)=n!/(n-r)!$, then divide by $r!$. Give the small example that ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, and CBA are the six orders of the same three-person group.
Worked example

A five-card hand with exactly two aces

The model A standard deck has $4$ aces and $48$ other cards.

A five-card hand has no order. To make a hand with exactly two aces:

  1. choose $2$ of the $4$ aces;
  2. choose $3$ of the $48$ other cards.
The count So the number of hands is $$\binom42\binom{48}{3}=6\cdot17{,}296=103{,}776.$$
Students often multiply by an unnecessary $5!$. A hand has no first card, second card, and so on, so both choices are combinations. The two stages produce disjoint card types, so the product rule connects them.

An identity, two ways

A useful identity: choose or leave out

Identity For $0\le k\le n$, $$\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n}{n-k}.$$
Algebraically $$\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!k!}.$$
By counting Choosing the $k$ people who attend determines the $n-k$ people who do not attend. Each choice determines the other.
Name the reversible matching: take a selected subset and send it to its complement. This is a bijection, so the two sets of choices have equal size. Both proofs are deliberately short and visible.
Double counting

Double counting: all subsets of an $n$-element set

By size For each $k=0,1,\ldots,n$, there are $\binom{n}{k}$ subsets with exactly $k$ elements. Add these numbers: $$\binom n0+\binom n1+\cdots+\binom nn.$$
By decisions For each of the $n$ elements, decide "in" or "out." This gives $2^n$ subsets.
Therefore Both methods count the set of all subsets of an $n$-element set, so $$\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}=2^n.$$
This is double counting: both methods count the same collection, not two different collections that happen to have the same answer. For $n=3$, students can list the eight subsets as a check.

Pascal's triangle and the binomial theorem

The same binomial coefficients that count groups also appear when we expand $(a+b)^n$.

We will first see the coefficients in Pascal's triangle, then explain why they occur.

3
The word "binomial" means an expression with two terms, such as $a+b$. Do not assume students know this vocabulary.
Pascal's triangle

Pascal's triangle

Definition Row $n$ contains the binomial coefficients $\binom n0,\binom n1,\ldots,\binom nn$.
Pascal's identity Every entry that is not at an edge is the sum of the two entries directly above it: $$\binom nk=\binom{n-1}{k-1}+\binom{n-1}{k}, \qquad 1\le k\le n-1.$$
$n$Coefficients
$0$$1$
$1$$1\quad1$
$2$$1\quad2\quad1$
$3$$1\quad3\quad3\quad1$
$4$$1\quad4\quad6\quad4\quad1$
$5$$1\quad5\quad10\quad10\quad5\quad1$
Use the row $n=4$ to read $\binom42=6$. The last formula is Pascal's identity; its usual combinatorial proof separates a $k$-person choice according to whether one particular person is selected.
The binomial theorem

The binomial theorem

Theorem For every non-negative integer $n$ (that is, $n=0,1,2,\ldots$) and real numbers $a,b$, $$(a+b)^n=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk a^{\,n-k}b^k.$$
Written out The expansion starts and ends as follows: $$(a+b)^n=\binom n0a^n+\binom n1a^{n-1}b+\cdots+\binom nn b^n.$$
Read the exponents The exponent of $a$ goes down from $n$ to $0$. The exponent of $b$ goes up from $0$ to $n$.
First check $n=2$: $(a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2$. The formula includes the endpoints because $\binom n0=\binom nn=1$ and $a^0=b^0=1$.
Worked expansion

Worked expansion: $(x+2)^5$

Pascal's row Pascal's row for $n=5$ is $$1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1.$$
So $$\begin{aligned} (x+2)^5 &=x^5+5x^4(2)+10x^3(2^2)\\ &\quad+10x^2(2^3)+5x(2^4)+2^5\\ &=x^5+10x^4+40x^3+80x^2+80x+32. \end{aligned}$$
Keep the unsimplified line visible long enough for students to trace the source of every term. In particular, the exponent on $x$ plus the exponent on $2$ is always $5$.
Why the coefficient

Why is the coefficient $\binom nk$?

Proof There are $n$ factors, each equal to $(a+b)$: $$(a+b)^n=(a+b)(a+b)\cdots(a+b).$$ To make the term $a^{n-k}b^k$:
  • choose $b$ from exactly $k$ factors;
  • choose $a$ from the other $n-k$ factors.

There are $\binom nk$ ways to choose those $k$ factors. So $\binom nk$ is the coefficient of $a^{n-k}b^k$.

Example In $(a+b)^3$, the term $ab^2$ comes from choosing $b$ from exactly two factors. The possible choices are factors $12$, $13$, and $23$, so its coefficient is $3=\binom32$.
Give the example $(a+b)^3$. The term $ab^2$ comes from choosing $b$ from exactly two factors; the possible choices are factors $12$, $13$, and $23$, so its coefficient is $3=\binom32$.

Walkthrough

The expansion Using the coefficients $1,4,6,4,1$, $$\begin{aligned} (2x-3)^4 &=(2x)^4+4(2x)^3(-3)+6(2x)^2(-3)^2\\ &\quad+4(2x)(-3)^3+(-3)^4\\ &=16x^4-96x^3+216x^2-216x+81. \end{aligned}$$

Pigeonhole

Pigeonhole principle

Definition If more than $m$ objects are put into $m$ boxes, at least one box has at least two objects.
Example Among $13$ people, at least two were born in the same month.
  • objects: the $13$ people;
  • boxes: the $12$ birth months;
  • since $13>12$, at least one month has at least two people.
Do not overstate it This shows a shared month, not necessarily a shared birthday.
This principle guarantees that a repeated category exists; it does not identify which category. The last sentence prevents a common overstatement of the conclusion.

Wrap-up

What today was about

  • Use the sum rule for separate alternatives and the product rule for steps in a process.
  • Use a permutation when order matters; use a combination when it does not.
  • $\binom nk$ counts selections of $k$ objects from $n$ distinct objects.
  • Pascal's triangle gives binomial coefficients, and the binomial theorem expands $(a+b)^n$.
  • Counting one collection in two ways can prove an identity.
